# Silicone traction spray on mecanum wheels — is it legal?

**Short answer: No, not as you're describing it.** Spraying your mecanum wheels with silicone traction spray so they contact the TILE floor is a rule violation under **R205**, and would be treated as FIELD damage under **G412** during a match. The manual doesn't name "silicone traction spray" by product, so the final call at your event rests with the LRI/Head Referee — but every applicable rule points the same way: don't do it.

## Why — the rules that apply

**R205 (Do not make a mess on the FIELD)** is the controlling rule:

> "ROBOTS may not contain any materials which if unintentionally released would damage the FIELD, other ROBOTS or delay the start of a MATCH due to required clean-up or decontamination. **Lubricants may be used only to reduce friction within the ROBOT and must not contaminate the FIELD or other ROBOTS.** ... Additional examples of items that will violate this rule include (but are not limited to): ... **liquid or gel materials**, **tire sealant**, and other lubricants including graphite powder."

The whole point of a traction spray is that it sits on the wheel's *contact surface* — the exact interface that presses against the tiles every rotation. So it isn't "within the ROBOT" and it isn't an accidental release: by design it transfers onto the field. That's contaminating the FIELD with a liquid/gel coating, which is precisely what R205 prohibits. The rule even lists "tire sealant" and "liquid or gel materials" as example violations, which is the same category as a sprayed-on wheel coating.

**G412 (Don't damage the FIELD)** is how it's enforced in a match. Its definition of FIELD damage explicitly includes:

> "contaminating the FIELD with a liquid or fine solid as in R205"

Penalty path: VERBAL WARNING → DISABLED if the Head Referee thinks more contamination is likely → YELLOW CARD for any subsequent occurrence, plus possible re-inspection before you can compete again. So beyond failing inspection, you can get disabled/carded mid-event.

**R201 (Do not damage the TILE floor)** is the related traction rule. It's about physical surface features (aggressive treads that gouge tiles) rather than coatings, so it's not the direct hit here — but it confirms the manual's stance that the wheel-to-tile interface is regulated and "extra grip at the tiles' expense" is the exact thing it guards against.

## Two practical points on top of the rules

1. **It's counterproductive anyway.** Silicone is a release agent / lubricant — on the tile surface it makes things *more* slippery, not less. You'd be reducing your own traction and coating the tiles so the next robot (and yours) slides.
2. **It's a fairness/safety issue.** Leaving slick residue on shared tiles degrades traction for every team that drives over that spot, which is squarely the kind of "contaminate the FIELD or other ROBOTS" harm R205 is written to prevent.

## The honest edge of the ambiguity

I'm confident the answer is "no" for silicone traction spray specifically, because silicone stays slick and transfers. The one genuinely gray area the manual doesn't resolve cleanly: a hypothetical surface treatment that fully cures/dries and leaves *zero* transferable residue. The rules don't explicitly bless or ban that, so it becomes an LRI judgment call. Silicone traction spray is not that product — it's wet, it transfers, and it reads as R205 contamination. If you had some specific cured coating you thought left no residue, that's a "check with your LRI / file an official Q&A before you build on it" situation, not something to assume is fine.

## What to do instead (legal grip options)

- Use grippier **wheel hardware** that's allowed on tiles (softer-durometer mecanum rollers, or drivetrain wheels within R201 — the point is the *material of the wheel itself*, not a sprayed coating that sheds).
- Fix grip through **weight distribution and drivetrain tuning** (more weight over the wheels, motor/gearing choices) rather than a surface additive.
- Clean your wheels (isopropyl alcohol wipe) before matches — clean rubber grips better than dusty rubber, and it leaves nothing on the field.
